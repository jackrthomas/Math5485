    Linear Algebra

    This appendix collects some facts from linear algebra that we use in the lectures on in Chapter 5: Linear systems of equations.

    Throughout, \(A \in \mathbb R^{m\times n}\) is a real matrix with entries \(A_{ij}\) (for \(1\leq i\leq m\) and \(1 \leq j \leq n\)), \(x \in \mathbb R^n\) are column vectors, \(e_j\) is the \(j^\text{th}\) standard basis vector (with \((e_j)_i = \delta_{ij}\)), and \(I\) is the identity matrix.

    Note

    The proofs on this page are non-examinable: they are included for completeness (click on “Proof” to expand them).

    Notation

    Basics

    • \(A^\top\) is the transpose of \(A\): \((A^\top)_{ij} = A_{ji}\). We have \((AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top\) (since \(\big((AB)^\top\big)_{ij} = \sum_k A_{jk} B_{ki} = (B^\top A^\top)_{ij}\)) and, if \(A\) is invertible, \((A^{-1})^\top = (A^\top)^{-1}\) (since \((A^{-1})^\top A^\top = (AA^{-1})^\top = I\) and \(A^\top (A^{-1})^\top = (A^{-1}A)^\top = I\)),
    • \(x^\top y = \sum_{j} x_j y_j\) is the dot product (or inner product) of \(x, y \in \mathbb R^n\). We say \(x\) and \(y\) are orthogonal if \(x^\top y = 0\),
    • \(|x| := \sqrt{x^\top x} = \sqrt{\sum_j x_j^2}\) is the Euclidean norm of \(x\). We say \(x\) is a unit vector if \(|x| = 1\), and \(u_1, \dots, u_k\) are orthonormal if \(u_i^\top u_j = 0\) for \(i\not= j\) and \(|u_j| = 1\) for all \(j\),
    • \(\|A\| := \max_{x\not=0} \frac{|Ax|}{|x|}\) is the operator norm of \(A\),

    Sets:

    • \(\ker A := \{ x \in \mathbb R^n : Ax = 0\}\) is the kernel (or null space) of \(A\),
    • \(\mathrm{range}\, A := \{Ax : x\in \mathbb R^n\}\) is its range: the span of the columns of \(A\),
    • \(\mathrm{null}\, A := \dim \mathrm{ker}\, A\) is the nullity of \(A\),
    • \(\mathrm{rank}\, A := \dim \mathrm{range}\, A\) is the rank of \(A\): the number of linearly independent columns (by Theorem 11, this is also the number of linearly independent rows),

    Spectrum:

    • \((\lambda, v)\) is an eigenpair of a square matrix \(A\) if \(Av = \lambda v\) and \(v\not=0\),
    • if the above is true, \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue and \(v\) is an eigenvector,
    • \(\sigma(A)\) is the set of eigenvalues of \(A\). This is the spectrum of \(A\).

    Matrices:

    • \(A\) is symmetric if \(A^\top = A\),
    • \(A\) is diagonal if \(A_{ij} = 0\) for \(i\not=j\),
    • \(A\) is upper triangular if \(A_{ij} = 0\) for \(i > j\),
    • \(A\) is lower triangular if \(A_{ij} = 0\) for \(i < j\),
    • \(A\) is unit lower triangular if \(A\) is lower triangular and \(A_{ii} = 1\) for all \(i\),
    • A square matrix \(Q\) is orthogonal if \(Q^\top Q = I\) (that is, \(Q^{-1} = Q^\top\)).

    Matrix multiplication

    Let \[ A = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & \cdots & a_p \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb R^{m\times p}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} b_1^\top \\ b_2^\top \\\vdots\\ b_p^\top \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb R^{p \times n} \] where \(a_k\) and \(b_k^\top\) is the \(k^\text{th}\) column of \(A\) and \(k^\text{th}\) row of \(B\), respectively. Then, for \(x \in \mathbb R^p\), we have that \[ Ax = \sum_{k=1}^p x_k a_k \] is a linear combination of the columns of \(A\). Moreover, the product of \(A\) and \(B\) is given by \[ \begin{align}\nonumber (AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^p A_{ik} B_{kj} % = \sum_{k=1}^p (a_k)_i (b_k)_j \end{align} \] That is, \(AB = \sum_{k} a_k b_k^\top\).

    Exercise 1 Show that, if \(u \in \mathbb R^m\) and \(v\in \mathbb R^n\) are non-zero, then \(u v^\top\) has rank \(1\).

    We have \((uv^\top)x = (v^\top x)\, u\), and so the range of \(uv^\top\) is \(\mathrm{span}\{u\}\), which is one dimensional.

    Invertible matrices

    Theorem 1 (Rank–nullity theorem) For \(A \in \mathbb R^{m\times n}\), we have \(\mathrm{rank}\, A + \dim \ker A = n\).

    Proof

    Proof. Let \(v_1, \dots, v_k\) be a basis of \(\ker A\) and extend it to a basis \(v_1, \dots, v_n\) of \(\mathbb R^n\). Since \(Av_i = 0\) for \(i \leq k\), every \(Ax = A \sum_i c_i v_i = \sum_{i > k} c_i A v_i\) is in the span of \(\{ A v_{k+1}, \dots, A v_n \}\), and so these vectors span \(\mathrm{range}\, A\).

    They are also linearly independent: if \(\sum_{i>k} c_i A v_i = 0\), then \(\sum_{i > k} c_i v_i \in \ker A\), so \(\sum_{i>k} c_i v_i = \sum_{i \leq k} d_i v_i\) for some \(d_i\), and \(c_i = 0\) for all \(i\) since \(v_1, \dots, v_n\) are linearly independent. Therefore, \(\mathrm{rank}\, A = n - k = n - \dim\ker A\).

    We define the determinant of \(A \in \mathbb R^{n\times n}\) by the Leibniz formula

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber \det A := \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^n A_{i \sigma(i)}, \end{align} \]

    where \(S_n\) is the set of permutations of \(\{1, \dots, n\}\) and \(\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \in \{\pm 1\}\) is the sign of \(\sigma\). Recall that every permutation is a product of transpositions (permutations that swap two indices), \(\mathrm{sgn}(\tau) = -1\) for a transposition \(\tau\), and \(\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma \circ \sigma') = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\,\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma')\).

    Theorem 2 (Properties of the determinant) For square \(A, B \in \mathbb R^{n\times n}\) and \(\alpha\in\mathbb R\),

    1. \(\det(AB) = \det A \det B\), and so \(\det(A^{-1}) = \frac1{\det A}\) if \(A\) is invertible,
    2. \(\det A^\top = \det A\) and \(\det (\alpha A) = \alpha^n \det A\),
    3. If \(A\) is (upper or lower) triangular, then \(\det A = \prod_{i=1}^n A_{ii}\),
    4. Swapping two rows (or columns) changes the sign of \(\det A\); adding a multiple of one row (or column) to another row (or column) does not change \(\det A\),
    5. If \(A\) has a row (or column) of zeros, then \(\det A = 0\).
    Proof

    Proof. We first note three facts coming from Leibniz formula:

    1. after multiplying a row of \(A\) by a constant \(\alpha\), the determinant is also multiplied by \(\alpha\) as each term in the sum contains exactly one entry of row \(i\).

    2. Swapping two rows changes the sign of the determinant. Let \(\tau\) be the transposition swapping rows \(i\) and \(j\), so the new matrix is \(A'_{k\ell} = A_{\tau(k)\ell}\). Substituting \(k = \tau(m)\) and \(\sigma' := \sigma \circ \tau\) (which runs over \(S_n\) as \(\sigma\) does, with \(\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma') = -\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\)), \[ \begin{align}\nonumber \det A' = \sum_{\sigma} \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_{m} A_{m\, \sigma(\tau(m))} = -\sum_{\sigma'} \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma') \prod_m A_{m \sigma'(m)} = -\det A. \end{align} \]

    3. If two rows of \(A\) are equal, then swapping them does not change \(A\), and so \(\det A = -\det A\) by (b). That is, \(\det A = 0\).

    We now prove the statements, starting with 2.

    2. Since \(\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\,\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma^{-1}) = \mathrm{sgn}(\mathrm{id}) = 1\), we have \(\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma^{-1}) = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\), and \(\sigma \mapsto \sigma^{-1}\) is a bijection of \(S_n\). Therefore, substituting \(j = \sigma(i)\), \[ \begin{align}\nonumber \det A^\top = \sum_\sigma \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_i A_{\sigma(i) i} = \sum_\sigma \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma^{-1}) \prod_j A_{j \sigma^{-1}(j)} = \det A. \end{align} \] Each term in the Leibniz formula is a product of \(n\) entries, and so \(\det(\alpha A) = \alpha^n \det A\). By \(\det A^\top = \det A\), every statement about rows below also holds for columns.

    3. Suppose \(A\) is upper triangular. A term \(\prod_i A_{i\sigma(i)}\) is non-zero only if \(\sigma(i) \geq i\) for all \(i\). Then \(\sigma(n) = n\), and thus \(\sigma(n-1) = n-1\), etc. and we must have \(\sigma = \mathrm{id}\). Therefore \(\det A = \prod_i A_{ii}\). For lower triangular \(A\), apply this to \(A^\top\).

    4. Swapping two rows is (b). Adding \(c\) times row \(j\) to row \(i\) gives, by (a), the determinant \(\det A + c \det A''\) where \(A''\) is \(A\) with row \(i\) replaced by row \(j\). Since \(A''\) has two equal rows, \(\det A'' = 0\) by (c).

    5. Each term in the Leibniz formula contains an entry of the zero row.

    1. Fix \(B\) and let \(D(A) := \det(AB)\). Since \((AB)_{ij} = \sum_k A_{ik} B_{kj} = a_i^\top B\) where \(a_{i}^\top\) is row \(i\) of \(A\). Therefore, (a)-(c) applied to \(AB\) show that \(D\) is linear in each row of \(A\), changes sign when two rows of \(A\) are swapped, and vanishes when two rows of \(A\) are equal. Writing \(a_i^\top = \sum_k A_{ik} e_k^\top\) and expanding using linearity in each row, \[ \begin{align}\nonumber D(A) = \sum_{k_1, \dots, k_n} A_{1k_1} \cdots A_{nk_n}\, D(E_{k_1 \cdots k_n}), \end{align} \] where \(E_{k_1 \cdots k_n}\) has rows \(e_{k_1}^\top, \dots, e_{k_n}^\top\). If two of the \(k_i\) are equal, then \(D(E_{k_1 \cdots k_n}) = 0\). Otherwise, \(k_i = \sigma(i)\) for some \(\sigma \in S_n\), and \(E_{k_1\cdots k_n}\) is obtained from \(I\) by swapping rows once for each transposition in \(\sigma\), so \(D(E_{k_1\cdots k_n}) = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) D(I)\). Therefore, \(D(A) = \det A \cdot D(I) = \det A \det B\). Finally, if \(A\) is invertible, then \(\det A \det A^{-1} = \det I = 1\) (by 3).

    Theorem 3 For a square matrix \(A \in \mathbb R^{n\times n}\), the following are equivalent:

    1. \(A\) is invertible (there exists \(A^{-1}\) with \(A^{-1}A = AA^{-1} = I\)),
    2. For every \(b \in \mathbb R^n\), the linear equation \(Ax = b\) has exactly one solution,
    3. \(\det A \not= 0\),
    4. \(\ker A = \{0\}\) (i.e. \(Ax = 0\) implies \(x = 0\)),
    5. \(\mathrm{rank}\, A = n\),
    6. \(\mathrm{null}\, A = 0\),
    7. The columns of \(A\) are linearly independent,
    8. We have \(0 \not\in \sigma(A)\) (i.e. \(0\) is not an eigenvalue of \(A\)).
    Proof

    Proof. We write \(a_1, \dots, a_n\) for the columns of \(A\), so that \(Ax = \sum_{k=1}^n x_k a_k\). First, we show that 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8 are all equivalent:

    • (4 \(\Leftrightarrow\) 6) By definition, \(\mathrm{null}\, A = \dim \ker A\), and a subspace has dimension \(0\) if and only if it is \(\{0\}\).
    • (4 \(\Leftrightarrow\) 7) The columns are linearly dependent if and only if \(\sum_k x_k a_k = 0\) for some \(x \not= 0\), i.e. if and only if \(Ax = 0\) for some \(x \not= 0\).
    • (4 \(\Leftrightarrow\) 8) \(0 \in \sigma(A)\) if and only if \(Av = 0 \cdot v = 0\) for some \(v \not= 0\).
    • (5 \(\Leftrightarrow\) 6) By Theorem 1, \(\mathrm{rank}\, A = n - \mathrm{null}\, A\).

    Next, we show \(1 \Rightarrow 2 \Rightarrow 4 \Rightarrow 1\):

    • (1 \(\Rightarrow\) 2) \(x = A^{-1}b\) is a solution since \(A(A^{-1}b) = b\). If \(Ax = Ay = b\), then \(x - y = A^{-1}A(x - y) = A^{-1}(b - b) = 0\), so the solution is unique.
    • (2 \(\Rightarrow\) 4) Taking \(b = 0\), the equation \(Ax = 0\) has exactly one solution. Since \(x = 0\) is a solution, it is the only one.
    • (4 \(\Rightarrow\) 1) By 5, \(\mathrm{range}\, A\) is an \(n\) dimensional subspace of \(\mathbb R^n\), and so \(\mathrm{range}\, A = \mathbb R^n\). Therefore, for each \(j\), there exists \(b_j\) with \(A b_j = e_j\). The matrix \(B := [b_1 | \cdots | b_n]\) then satisfies \(AB = I\). Moreover, \(A(BA - I) = (AB)A - A = 0\), so every column of \(BA - I\) is in \(\ker A = \{0\}\). That is, \(BA = I\) and \(B = A^{-1}\).

    Finally, we show \(1 \Rightarrow 3 \Rightarrow 4\):

    • (1 \(\Rightarrow\) 3) By Theorem 2, \(\det A \det A^{-1} = \det (AA^{-1}) = \det I = 1\), and so \(\det A \not= 0\).
    • (3 \(\Rightarrow\) 4) Suppose that 4 is not satisfied: \(Ax = 0\) for some \(x \not= 0\). Choose \(k\) with \(x_k \not= 0\) and, for each \(j \not= k\), add \(\frac{x_j}{x_k}\) times column \(j\) to column \(k\). By Theorem 2, this does not change the determinant. The new \(k^\text{th}\) column is \(a_k + \sum_{j\not=k} \frac{x_j}{x_k} a_j = \frac{1}{x_k} Ax = 0\), and so (again by Theorem 2) \(\det A = 0\). That is, 3 is not satisfied.

    Therefore, all of the statements are equivalent.

    Eigenvalues

    The eigenvalues of \(A \in \mathbb R^{n\times n}\) are the roots of the characteristic polynomial \(p(\lambda) := \det(\lambda I - A)\), a monic polynomial of degree \(n\) (since \(Av = \lambda v\) for some \(v \not=0\) if and only if \(\ker(\lambda I - A) \not= \{0\}\), i.e. if and only if \(\det(\lambda I - A) = 0\) by Theorem 3). Therefore, \(A\) has \(n\) eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n\) (counted with multiplicity), which may be complex, and

    \[ \begin{align} \det A &= \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda_i, \nonumber\\ \mathrm{Tr}\, A &:= \sum_{i=1}^n A_{ii} = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i. \end{align} \]

    Proof

    Proof. By the Leibniz formula, \(p(\lambda) = \sum_\sigma \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_i \big(\lambda \delta_{i\sigma(i)} - A_{i\sigma(i)}\big)\). If \(\sigma \not= \mathrm{id}\), then \(\sigma(i) \not= i\) for at least two \(i\), so the corresponding term is a polynomial of degree at most \(n - 2\). Therefore, \[ \begin{align}\nonumber p(\lambda) = \prod_{i=1}^n (\lambda - A_{ii}) + (\text{degree} \leq n-2) = \lambda^n - (\mathrm{Tr}\, A)\, \lambda^{n-1} + \cdots, \end{align} \] which is monic of degree \(n\). By the fundamental theorem of algebra, \(p(\lambda) = \prod_{i=1}^n (\lambda - \lambda_i)\), whose coefficient of \(\lambda^{n-1}\) is \(-\sum_i \lambda_i\), so \(\mathrm{Tr}\, A = \sum_i \lambda_i\). Moreover, \((-1)^n \prod_i \lambda_i = p(0) = \det(-A) = (-1)^n \det A\) by Theorem 2.

    Theorem 4 (Similar matrices) If \(S\) is invertible, then \(A\) and \(S^{-1}AS\) have the same eigenvalues. Moreover, \((\lambda, v)\) is an eigenpair of \(A\) if and only if \((\lambda, S^{-1}v)\) is an eigenpair of \(S^{-1}AS\).

    Proof

    Proof. By Theorem 2, \(\det(\lambda I - S^{-1}AS) = \det\big(S^{-1}(\lambda I - A)S\big) = \det(S^{-1})\det(\lambda I - A) \det S = \det(\lambda I - A)\). Also, \(S^{-1}AS (S^{-1}v) = S^{-1}Av\), which equals \(\lambda S^{-1}v\) if and only if \(Av = \lambda v\).

    Triangular and permutation matrices

    Theorem 5 (Triangular matrices)  

    1. If \(L\) and \(M\) are lower triangular, then \(LM\) is lower triangular,
    2. If \(L\) and \(M\) are unit lower triangular, then \(LM\) is unit lower triangular,
    3. A triangular matrix \(L\) is invertible if and only if its diagonal entries are all non-zero,
    4. If \(L\) is invertible and lower triangular, then \(L^{-1}\) is triangular (and unit lower triangular if \(L\) is),
    5. The eigenvalues of a lower triangular matrix are its diagonal entries.

    The same holds with “lower” replaced by “upper”.

    Proof

    Proof. We have \((LM)_{ij} = \sum_k L_{ik} M_{kj}\) and a term in this sum is non-zero only if \(k \leq i\) and \(j \leq k\). Therefore, \((LM)_{ij} = 0\) if \(j > i\), and \((LM)_{ii} = L_{ii} M_{ii}\). If \(L\) and \(M\) are unit triangular, then the diagonal is \((LM)_{ii} = 1 \cdot 1 = 1\) and \(LM\) is unit triangular.

    By Theorem 2, \(\det L = \prod_i L_{ii}\), which is non-zero if and only if every \(L_{ii} \not= 0\). By Theorem 3, this is the case if and only if \(L\) is invertible. In this case, the \(j^\text{th}\) column \(x\) of \(L^{-1}\) solves \(Lx = e_j\). Forward substitution gives \(x_1 = \cdots = x_{j-1} = 0\) and \(x_j = \frac1{L_{jj}}\), and so \(L^{-1}\) is lower triangular with diagonal entries \(\frac1{L_{jj}}\).

    \(\lambda I - L\) is triangular with diagonal entries \(\lambda - L_{ii}\), and so \(\det(\lambda I - L) = \prod_i (\lambda - L_{ii})\). The roots of this equation are exactly the eigenvalues of \(L\).

    A permutation matrix \(P\) is the identity matrix with its rows reordered: \(P\) has exactly one \(1\) in each row and each column and zeros elsewhere. If the \(i^\text{th}\) row of \(P\) is \(e_{p(i)}^T\), then \((PA)_{ij} = A_{p(i) j}\): multiplying by \(P\) on the left reorders the rows of \(A\). Permutation matrices are orthogonal: the columns of \(P\) are distinct standard basis vectors, so \((P^\top P)_{ij} = e_{p(i)}^\top e_{p(j)} = \delta_{ij}\) (where now \(e_{p(j)}\) is the \(j^\text{th}\) column), i.e. \(P^\top P = I\) and \(P^{-1} = P^\top\).

    Orthogonal matrices

    Theorem 6 (Orthogonal matrices)  

    1. \(Q \in \mathbb R^{m\times n}\) has orthonormal columns if and only if \(Q^\top Q = I\).
    2. If \(m = n\), and \(Q^\top Q = I\), then \(QQ^T = I\),
    3. If \(Q\) has orthonormal columns, then \((Qx)^T(Qy) = x^Ty\) and \(|Qx| = |x|\) for all \(x, y\),
    4. The product of orthogonal matrices is orthogonal, and if \(Q\) is orthogonal then so is \(Q^T = Q^{-1}\),
    5. If \(Q\) is orthogonal then \(\det Q = \pm 1\) and every eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of \(Q\) has \(|\lambda| = 1\).
    Proof

    Proof. We have \((Q^\top Q)_{ij} = q_i^\top q_j\) where \(q_j\) is the \(j^\text{th}\) column of \(Q\). If \(m = n\) and \(Q^\top Q = I\), then \(Qx = 0\) implies \(x = Q^\top Q x = 0\), so \(Q\) is invertible by Theorem 3, and \(Q^{-1} = (Q^\top Q) Q^{-1} = Q^\top\). Therefore, \(QQ^\top = QQ^{-1} = I\).

    We have \((Qx)^T(Qy) = x^TQ^TQy = x^Ty\) and, taking \(y = x\), gives \(|Qx| = |x|\).

    We have \((Q_1Q_2)^T(Q_1Q_2) = Q_2^T Q_1^T Q_1 Q_2 = Q_2^TQ_2 = I\), and \((Q^T)^TQ^T = QQ^T = I\).

    Since \(\det I = 1\), we have \(1 = \det(Q^TQ) = (\det Q)^2\). If \(Qv = \lambda v\) with \(v \in \mathbb C^n\) non-zero, then \(|\lambda|^2 \bar v^\top v = \overline{(Qv)}^\top Qv = \bar v^\top Q^\top Q v = \bar v^\top v\) (as \(Q\) is real), so \(|\lambda| = 1\).

    Theorem 7 (Orthonormal bases) Any orthonormal vectors \(u_1, \dots, u_k \in \mathbb R^m\) can be extended to an orthonormal basis \(u_1, \dots, u_m\) of \(\mathbb R^m\). Equivalently, any matrix with orthonormal columns can be extended (by adding columns) to an orthogonal matrix.

    Symmetric matrices

    Theorem 8 (Spectral theorem) A real matrix \(A \in \mathbb R^{n\times n}\) is symmetric if and only if there exist an orthogonal matrix \(P\) and a (real) diagonal matrix \(\Lambda\) such that

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber A = P \Lambda P^T. \end{align} \]

    The diagonal entries \(\lambda_i := \Lambda_{ii}\) are the eigenvalues of \(A\) and the columns \(p_1, \dots, p_n\) of \(P\) are an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb R^n\) consisting of eigenvectors of \(A\). That is, \(A = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i\, p_i p_i^T\).

    In particular, if \(A\) is symmetric then

    • every eigenvalue of \(A\) is real,
    • eigenvectors corresponding to different eigenvalues are orthogonal: if \(Au = \lambda u\) and \(Av = \mu v\) with \(\lambda \not= \mu\), then \(\lambda u^Tv = (Au)^Tv = u^TAv = \mu u^Tv\) and so \(u^Tv = 0\).

    More generally, a (possibly complex) matrix \(A\) is normal (i.e. \(A^\star A = AA^\star\), where \(A^\star := \bar A^T\) is the conjugate transpose) if and only if \(A = U\Lambda U^\star\) for some unitary \(U\) (i.e. \(U^\star U = I\)) and diagonal \(\Lambda\) (which may now be complex).

    Definition 1 (Positive definite matrices) A symmetric matrix \(A \in \mathbb R^{n\times n}\) is positive semi-definite if \(x^TAx \geq 0\) for all \(x\in\mathbb R^n\), and positive definite if \(x^TAx > 0\) for all \(x\not=0\).

    By Theorem 8, \(x^TAx = (P^Tx)^T \Lambda (P^Tx) = \sum_i \lambda_i (p_i^Tx)^2\), and so a symmetric matrix is positive semi-definite (respectively definite) if and only if all of its eigenvalues are \(\geq 0\) (respectively \(> 0\)).

    Proof

    Proof. If every \(\lambda_i \geq 0\), then \(x^TAx = \sum_i \lambda_i (p_i^Tx)^2 \geq 0\). If every \(\lambda_i > 0\) and \(x \not= 0\), then \(P^Tx \not= 0\) (as \(P^T\) is invertible), so some \(p_i^Tx \not= 0\) and \(x^TAx > 0\). Conversely, taking \(x = p_i\) gives \(x^TAx = \lambda_i\).

    Example 1 For any \(A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\), the matrix \(A^TA \in \mathbb R^{n\times n}\) is symmetric (since \((A^TA)^T = A^T (A^T)^T = A^TA\)) and positive semi-definite since \(x^TA^TAx = |Ax|^2 \geq 0\). Therefore, its eigenvalues are real and \(\geq 0\). Moreover, \(\ker(A^TA) = \ker A\): if \(A^TAx = 0\), then \(|Ax|^2 = x^TA^TAx = 0\) and so \(Ax = 0\) (and conversely \(Ax = 0\) implies \(A^TAx = 0\)). Therefore, by Theorem 3, \(A^TA\) is invertible if and only if \(\ker A = \{0\}\), i.e. if and only if \(A\) has linearly independent columns (as \(Ax = \sum_k x_k a_k\)). In this case, \(A^TA\) is positive definite since \(x^TA^TAx = |Ax|^2 > 0\) for \(x \not= 0\).

    Norms

    Theorem 9 (Cauchy–Schwarz inequality) For all \(x, y \in \mathbb R^n\), we have \(|x^Ty| \leq |x|\,|y|\), with equality if and only if \(x\) and \(y\) are parallel.

    Proof

    Proof. If \(y = 0\) there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, for all \(t\in\mathbb R\),

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber 0 \leq |x - ty|^2 = |x|^2 - 2t\, x^Ty + t^2 |y|^2. \end{align} \]

    Choosing \(t = x^Ty/|y|^2\) gives \(0 \leq |x|^2 - (x^Ty)^2/|y|^2\), with equality if and only if \(x = ty\).

    As a consequence, the Euclidean norm is a norm: \(|x| \geq 0\) with \(|x| = 0\) if and only if \(x = 0\), \(|\alpha x| = |\alpha|\,|x|\) and (the triangle inequality)

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber |x + y|^2 = |x|^2 + 2x^Ty + |y|^2 \leq \big(|x| + |y|\big)^2. \end{align} \]

    Theorem 10 (Properties of the operator norm) For \(A, B\) of compatible sizes,

    1. \(\|A\| = \max_{|x| = 1} |Ax|\) and \(|Ax| \leq \|A\|\,|x|\) for all \(x\),
    2. \(\|\cdot\|\) is a norm on \(\mathbb R^{m\times n}\), \(\|I\| = 1\) and \(\|AB\| \leq \|A\|\,\|B\|\),
    3. if \(P\) and \(Q\) are orthogonal, then \(\|PAQ\| = \|A\|\),
    4. \(|\lambda| \leq \|A\|\) for every eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of a square matrix \(A\),
    5. if \(A\) is symmetric with eigenvalues \((\lambda_i)\), then \(\|A\| = \max_i |\lambda_i|\),
    6. \(\|A^T\| = \|A\|\) and \(\|A^TA\| = \|A\|^2\). Therefore, \(\|A\| = \sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(A^TA)}\), where \(\lambda_{\max}(A^TA) \geq 0\) is the largest eigenvalue of \(A^TA\) (see Example 1 and 5).

    Singular value decomposition

    Theorem 11 (Singular value decomposition) Every \(A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\) can be written as \(A = P\Sigma Q^T\) where \(P\in\mathbb R^{m\times m}\) and \(Q\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}\) are orthogonal and \(\Sigma\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\) is diagonal with entries \(\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_r \geq 0\) (\(r := \min\{m,n\}\)), the singular values of \(A\). Moreover,

    1. \(\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_r^2\) are the \(r\) largest eigenvalues of \(A^TA\) (and of \(AA^T\)),
    2. \(\mathrm{rank}\, A = \mathrm{rank}\, A^T\) is the number of non-zero singular values (so the number of linearly independent columns of \(A\) is equal to the number of linearly independent rows),
    3. \(A = \sum_{j=1}^r \sigma_j u_j v_j^T\) where \(u_j\) and \(v_j\) are the \(j^\text{th}\) columns of \(P\) and \(Q\),
    4. \(\|A\| = \sigma_1\) and, if \(A\) is square and invertible, \(\|A^{-1}\| = 1/\sigma_n\) and so the condition number \(\kappa(A) := \|A\|\,\|A^{-1}\|\) (Lecture 14) is \(\kappa(A) = \sigma_1/\sigma_n\).
