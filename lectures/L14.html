    using Plots, LaTeXStrings, LinearAlgebra

function forwardSub(L, b)
    n = size(L,1)
    y = zeros(n)
    y[1] = b[1] / L[1, 1]
    for i ∈ 2:n
        s = sum(L[i, j] * y[j] for j ∈ 1:i-1)
        y[i] = (b[i] - s) / L[i, i]
    end
    return y
end

function backwardSub(U, y)
    n = size(U, 1)
    x = zeros(n)
    x[n] = y[n] / U[n, n]
    for i ∈ n-1:-1:1
        s = sum(U[i, j] * x[j] for j ∈ i+1:n)
        x[i] = (y[i] - s) / U[i, i]
    end
    return x
end

    14.1 Previously…..

    • Cramer’s rule requires \(O(n!)\) operations: far too expensive,
    • Gaussian elimination (elementary row operations) requires \(O(n^3)\) operations,
    • If \(L\) is unit lower triangular and \(U\) is upper triangular, then we solve \(LUx = b\) by solving \(Ly = b\) (forwards substitution) and then \(Ux = y\) (backwards substitution): this requires \(O(n^2)\) operations.

    14.2 Computing LU decomposition

    Algorithm: set \(A^{(1)} := A\) and, for \(k = 1, \dots, n\), let \(u_k\) be the \(k\)-th row of \(A^{(k)}\), \(\ell_k\) the \(k\)-th column of \(A^{(k)}\) divided by \((u_k)_k\), and \[ \begin{align} A^{(k+1)} := A^{(k)} - \ell_k u_k. \end{align} \] Then \(A = LU\) where \(L\) has columns \(\ell_k\) and \(U\) has rows \(u_k\).

    Code 
    function LU_decomp( A )
    n = size(A, 1)      
    L = diagm(ones(n))  
    U = zeros(n, n)

    for j ∈ 1:n-1
        U[j, :] = A[j, :]
        L[:, j] = A[:, j] / U[j, j]
        A = A - L[:, j] * U[j, :]'
    end
    U[n, n] = A[n, n]

    return tril(L), triu(U)
end

    Exercise 14.1 (Warm-up) Use LU decomposition combined with forward and backwards substitution to solve the linear system of equations

    \[ \begin{align} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 & 10 \\ 4 & 8 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x\\y\\z \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}. \nonumber \end{align} \]

    Step 1. \(u_1 = (2,3,4)\) and \(\ell_1 = (1, 2, 2)^\intercal\) (the first column divided by \(2\)). Then \[ A^{(2)} = A - \ell_1 u_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2&3&4\\4&5&10\\4&8&2\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 2&3&4\\4&6&8\\4&6&8\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0&0&0\\0&-1&2\\0&2&-6\end{pmatrix}. \] Step 2. \(u_2 = (0,-1,2)\) and \(\ell_2 = (0, 1, -2)^\intercal\) (the second column divided by \(-1\)), so that \(A^{(3)} = A^{(2)} - \ell_2 u_2 = \mathrm{diag}(0,0,-2)\).

    Step 3. \(u_3 = (0,0,-2)\) and \(\ell_3 = (0,0,1)^\intercal\). Hence \[ L = \begin{pmatrix} 1&0&0\\2&1&0\\2&-2&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad U = \begin{pmatrix} 2&3&4\\0&-1&2\\0&0&-2\end{pmatrix}. \]

    Forwards substitution (\(Ly = b\)): \(y_1 = 1\), \(y_2 = 0 - 2y_1 = -2\), \(y_3 = -1 - 2y_1 + 2y_2 = -7\).

    Backwards substitution (\(U(x,y,z)^\intercal = (y_1,y_2,y_3)^\intercal\)): \(z = \frac{-7}{-2} = \frac72\), then \(-y + 2z = -2\) gives \(y = 9\), and \(2x + 3y + 4z = 1\) gives \(x = \frac{1 - 27 - 14}{2} = -20\).

    So \((x,y,z) = (-20, 9, \frac72)\). Julia’s lu(A) (below) returns a different factorisation because it pivots (see below): it chooses the largest entry, \(4\), in the first column as the first pivot.

    Exercise 14.3 What happens when you apply LU decomposition to the following matrix?

    \[ \begin{align} A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 4 & 3 \\ -2 & 0 & 2 & -13 \\ 1 & 15 & 2 & -4.5 \\ -4 & 5 & -7 & -10 \end{pmatrix}. \nonumber \end{align} \]

    The first row of \(U\) is \(u_1 = (2, 0, 4, 3)\) and so the first column of \(L\) is \(\ell_1 = (1, -1, 1/2, -2)^\intercal\) (the first column of \(A\) divided by \((u_1)_1\)). Therefore, we have

    \[ \begin{align} A^{(2)} := A - \ell_1 u_1 &= \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 4 & 3 \\ -2 & 0 & 2 & -13 \\ 1 & 15 & 2 & -4.5 \\ -4 & 5 & -7 & -10 \end{pmatrix} % - \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 4 & 3 \\ -2 & 0 & -4 & -3 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 & 3/2 \\ -4 & 0 & -8 & -6 \end{pmatrix} \nonumber\\ % &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 6 & -10 \\ 0 & 15 & 0 & -6 \\ 0 & 5 & 1 & -4 \end{pmatrix}. \nonumber \end{align} \]

    Now we have a problem! In the algorithm above, we would set \(u_2 = (0, 0, 6, -10)\) and then let \(\ell_2\) be the second column of \(A^{(2)}\) divided by \((u_2)_2\) but this is zero!

    This is not just bad luck: \(A\) has no factorisation \(A = LU\) (with \(L\) unit lower triangular and \(U\) upper triangular) at all. If \(A = LU\), then the leading \(2\times 2\) blocks satisfy \(A_{1:2,1:2} = L_{1:2,1:2} U_{1:2,1:2}\) and so \[ u_{11}u_{22} = \det\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0\\ -2 & 0\end{pmatrix} = 0. \] However, \(\det A = \det L \det U = u_{11}u_{22}u_{33}u_{44}\) and \(\det A = 60 \not= 0\), a contradiction.

    The fix is to pivot (see below): at step 2, use the row of \(A^{(2)}\) with the largest entry (in absolute value) in column 2, which is row 3. This gives \(u_2 = (0,15,0,-6)\) and \(\ell_2 = (0,0,1,\frac13)^\intercal\). At step 3 the largest entry in column 3 of \(A^{(3)} = A^{(2)} - \ell_2 u_2\) is \(6\) in row 2, so \(u_3 = (0,0,6,-10)\), \(\ell_3 = (0,1,0,\frac16)^\intercal\), and finally \(u_4 = (0,0,0,-\frac13)\), \(\ell_4 = (0,0,0,1)^\intercal\) with \(p = (1,3,2,4)\): \[ PL = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0\\ \tfrac12 & 1 & 0 & 0\\ -1 & 0 & 1 & 0\\ -2 & \tfrac13 & \tfrac16 & 1\end{pmatrix},\qquad U = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 4 & 3\\ 0 & 15 & 0 & -6\\ 0 & 0 & 6 & -10\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & -\tfrac13\end{pmatrix},\qquad PA = (PL)\,U . \] (Julia’s lu(A) pivots from the very first step, choosing \(-4\) in row 4, and so returns a different, equally valid, factorisation with \(p = (4,3,2,1)\).)

    14.3 Pivotting

    Before, we chose \(u_k\) to be the \(k\)-th row of \(A^{(k)}\). Now we instead choose the \(p(k)\) row so that we maximise the absolute value of \([A^{(k)}]_{p(k),k}\). Let’s consider an example:

    Example 14.1 Use LU decomposition to solve \(Ax = b\) where \(b = (0, 1, -1)^\intercal\) and

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}. \end{align} \]

    First, we choose \(p(1)\) so that \(|A_{p(1),1}|\) is maximised: that is, \(p(1) = 3\). Then, we let \(u_1\) be the \(p(1)\) row of \(A\): that is, \(u_1 = (2, 0, 3)\) and \(\ell_1\) be the first column of \(A\) divided by \((u_1)_1=2\): that is, \(\ell_1 = ( 1/2, 0, 1 )^\intercal\). At this stage we have

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber L = \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & \bullet & \bullet \\ 0 & \bullet & \bullet \\ 1 & \bullet & \bullet \end{pmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 3 \\ \bullet & \bullet & \bullet \\ \bullet & \bullet & \bullet \end{pmatrix}, \qquad p(1) = 3. \end{align} \]

    Notice that \(L\) is no longer going to be lower triangular, but we will fix that at the end (this is why we are tracking the permutation \(p\)).

    Then we consider the matrix \(A^{(2)} := A - \ell_1 u_1\) and repeat the same procedure:

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber A^{(2)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 3/2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix} % = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{align} \]

    We choose \(p(2)\) to maximise \(|A^{(2)}_{p(2),2}|\) and so we set \(p(2) = 2\) and \(u_2 = (0, 2, 1)\). Then we set \(\ell_2\) to be the second column of \(A^{(2)}\) divided by \((u_2)_2 = 2\) (that is, \(\ell_2 = (0, 1, 0)^\intercal\)) and we have

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber L = \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & 0 & \bullet \\ 0 & 1 & \bullet \\ 1 & 0 & \bullet \end{pmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ \bullet & \bullet & \bullet \end{pmatrix}, \qquad p(1) = 3, p(2) = 2. \end{align} \]

    Then we consider the matrix \(A^{(3)} := A^{(2)} - \ell_2 u_2\) and repeat the same procedure:

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber A^{(3)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} % = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \end{align} \]

    and conclude

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber L = \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad U = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1/2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad p(1) = 3, p(2) = 2, p(3)=1. \end{align} \]

    Now, we want to solve \(A x = b\). Notice that

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber PL = \big( L_{p(i),j} \big) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1/2 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \end{align} \]

    is lower triangular (where \(P\) is the permutation matrix with a \(1\) in the \((i,p(i))\) entry). Since \(A = LU\), we have \(PA = (PL)U\) and so \(Ax = b\) is equivalent to

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber (PL) U x = Pb \end{align} \]

    which we may solve using forwards and backwards substitution as before.

    Exercise 14.4 Use the PLU decomposition as above to solve

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix}. \end{align} \]

    \(Pb = (b_{p(1)}, b_{p(2)}, b_{p(3)})^\intercal\).

    14.4 Why use the largest pivot?

    Answer: We get better conditioning by choosing the largest pivot. Later, we introduce the condition number of a matrix, written \(\kappa(A)\) (Definition 14.2), which will make the following discussion rigorous.

    Suppose we have the matrix

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber M(\epsilon )= \begin{pmatrix} -\epsilon & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \end{align} \]

    with \(|\epsilon| \ll 1\).

    1. It turns out that this matrix is well conditioned for all small \(\epsilon\). Here, we can see the condition number is bounded for all small \(\epsilon\):
    Code 
    M(ϵ) = [-ϵ 1; 1 -1]
plot( ϵ->cond(M(ϵ)), -1/2, 1/2, title="Condition number of M", xlabel="ϵ")
    1. If we did not use pivoting, the LU decomposition of \(M(\epsilon)\) is

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -\epsilon^{-1} & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} -\epsilon & 1 \\ 0 & \epsilon^{-1}-1 \end{pmatrix}. \end{align} \]

    Both of these matrices are ill-conditioned. In the following, we can see that the condition numbers explode near \(\epsilon \approx 0\).

    Code 
    plot( ϵ->cond([1 0 ; -1/ϵ 1 ]), 0, 1/2, yaxis = :log10, title="Condition numbers", xlabel="ϵ", label="κ(L)")
plot!( ϵ->cond([-ϵ 1; 0 -1+1/ϵ]), 0, 1/2, label="κ(U)")

    Notice this is not a problem with \(M(\epsilon)\), but with our choice of LU decomposition. That is, without pivoting, our algorithm is unstable.

    1. Using PLU decomposition, we arrive at

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber LU = \begin{pmatrix} -\epsilon & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1-\epsilon \end{pmatrix} \end{align} \]

    (with \(p(1) = 2\) and \(p(2) = 1\)). These factors are again well conditioned:

    Code 
    plot( ϵ->cond([-ϵ 1 ; 1 0 ]), -1/2, 1/2, title="Condition numbers", xlabel="ϵ", label="κ(L)")
plot!( ϵ->cond([1 -1; 0 1-ϵ]), -1/2, 1/2, label="κ(U)")

    14.5 Condition numbers of linear systems of equations

    In order to fully understand the previous section, we need to make sense of the condition number \(\kappa(A)\). Recall (Definition 3.1) that when we were considering functions \(f : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R\), the condition number was (approximately) the constant of proportionality between the relative error in the input to the relative error of the output. That is, if \(x \approx \tilde{x}\) then, we have

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber \frac{|f(x)- f(\tilde{x})|}{|f(x)|} \approx \kappa_f(x) \frac{|x- \tilde{x}|}{|x|}. \end{align} \]

    We now take this idea but the “input” is the vector \(b\) and the “output” is \(x\) solving the linear system \(A x = b\). Again, the condition number measures how sensitive the solution \(x\) is to errors in the data \(b\). Roughly speaking, if the condition number is \(10^\rho\), we expect to lose \(\rho\) digits of accuracy when we go from \(b\) to \(x\). This made perfect sense on \(\mathbb R\) (with the absolute value) but now we have many choices for the norm. We shall fix the Euclidean norm for the remainder of the course:

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber |x| := \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^n |x_j|^2}. \end{align} \]

    We also need the corresponding operator norm on the space of matrices:

    Definition 14.1 (Operator norm) \[ \begin{align}\nonumber \|A\| := \max_{x\not=0} \frac{|Ax|}{|x|}. \end{align} \]

    Notice that, we have \(|Ax| \leq \|A\| |x|\) for all \(x\). We are now ready to define the condition number of the matrix \(A\) (or the corresponding linear system).

    Suppose we have the exact data \(b\) and exact solution \(x\) (solving \(Ax=b\)) and we have the approximate data \(\tilde{b}\) (for example, the floating point representation of \(b\)) and corresponding solution \(\tilde{x}\) solving \(A\tilde{x} = \tilde{b}\). First, notice that \(|b| = |Ax| \leq \|A\| |x|\) and that \(A(x - \tilde{x}) = b - \tilde{b}\) and so

    \[ \begin{align}\nonumber |x - \tilde{x}| \leq \|A^{-1}\| |b - \tilde{b}|. \end{align} \]

    Therefore, we have

    \[ \begin{align} \frac {|x-\tilde{x}|/|x|} {|b-\tilde{b}|/|b|} % = \frac {|x-\tilde{x}|} {|b-\tilde{b}|} \frac {|b|} {|x|} % \leq \|A^{-1}\| \|A\|. \end{align} \tag{14.1}\]

    Definition 14.2 (Condition number of a matrix) The condition number of an invertible matrix \(A\) is the right hand side of (14.1): \[ \begin{align}\nonumber \kappa(A) := \|A^{-1}\| \|A\|. \end{align} \]

    Exercise 14.5  

    1. Use the definitions directly to show that the following matrices are ill-conditioned for small \(\epsilon > 0\), \[ \begin{align}\nonumber \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -\epsilon^{-1} & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix} -\epsilon & 1 \\ 0 & \epsilon^{-1}-1 \end{pmatrix}, \end{align} \]
    2. Show that \(\|A\| = \max_{|x|=1} |Ax|\),
    3. Show that \(\|AB\| \leq \|A\|\|B\|\) and hence \(\|A^k\| \leq \|A\|^k\),
    4. What is \(\|D\|\) for diagonal \(D\)?
    5. A real matrix \(A\) is symmetric (i.e. \(A^T = A\)) if and only if there exists orthogonal \(P\) (i.e. \(P^{-1} = P^T\)) and diagonal \(\Lambda\) such that \(A = P\Lambda P^T\). Show that the columns of \(P\) are eigenvectors of \(A\),
    6. Show that, for symmetric matrices \(A\), the operator norm \(\|A\|\) is equal to \(\max_i |\lambda_i|\) where \((\lambda_i)\) are the eigenvalues of \(A\).

    For 1, \(\|A\| \geq |Ae_j|\) for each standard basis vector \(e_j\); apply this to \(A\) and to \(A^{-1}\). For 6, use that \(|Px| = |x|\) for orthogonal \(P\).

    1. Let \(0 < \epsilon < 1\). For \(L_\epsilon = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ -\epsilon^{-1} & 1\end{pmatrix}\) we have \(L_\epsilon^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0\\ \epsilon^{-1} & 1\end{pmatrix}\) and so \[ \|L_\epsilon\| \geq |L_\epsilon e_1| = \sqrt{1 + \epsilon^{-2}}, \qquad \|L_\epsilon^{-1}\| \geq |L_\epsilon^{-1}e_1| = \sqrt{1+\epsilon^{-2}}, \] which means \(\kappa(L_\epsilon) \geq 1 + \epsilon^{-2} \to \infty\) as \(\epsilon\to0\). For \(U_\epsilon = \begin{pmatrix} -\epsilon & 1\\ 0 & \epsilon^{-1} - 1\end{pmatrix}\), we have \[ U_\epsilon^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} -\epsilon^{-1} & \frac{1}{1-\epsilon}\\ 0 & \frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}\end{pmatrix} \] and so \(\|U_\epsilon\| \geq |U_\epsilon e_2| > \epsilon^{-1} - 1\) and \(\|U_\epsilon^{-1}\| \geq |U_\epsilon^{-1} e_1| = \epsilon^{-1}\). Therefore, \(\kappa(U_\epsilon) > \epsilon^{-1}(\epsilon^{-1} - 1) \to\infty\). (In contrast, \(\kappa(M(\epsilon)) \to \frac{3+\sqrt5}{2} \approx 2.618\) as \(\epsilon \to 0\), by part 6.)

    2. For \(x\not= 0\), \(\hat x := x/|x|\) satisfies \(|\hat x| = 1\) and \(\frac{|Ax|}{|x|} = |A\hat x|\). Therefore, the set of values \(\{ |Ax|/|x| : x \not= 0 \}\) is equal to \(\{ |Ay| : |y| = 1\}\) and so the maxima agree.

    3. For all \(x\), \(|ABx| \leq \|A\|\,|Bx| \leq \|A\|\,\|B\|\,|x|\). Dividing by \(|x|\) and maximising gives \(\|AB\|\leq\|A\|\|B\|\). By induction, \(\|A^{k}\| \leq \|A\|\,\|A^{k-1}\| \leq \|A\|^k\).

    4. Let \(D = \mathrm{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_n)\). Then \(|Dx|^2 = \sum_i d_i^2x_i^2 \leq (\max_i |d_i|)^2|x|^2\) and so \(\|D\| \leq \max_i|d_i|\). Choosing \(x = e_{i^\star}\) where \(|d_{i^\star}| = \max_i |d_i|\) shows that \(\|D\| = \max_i|d_i|\).

    5. If \(A = P\Lambda P^T\) with \(P^T = P^{-1}\), then \(AP = P\Lambda\). The \(i\)-th column of this equation reads \(A p_i = \Lambda_{ii} p_i\) where \(p_i\) is the \(i\)-th column of \(P\). Since \(P\) is invertible, \(p_i \not= 0\) and so \(p_i\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda_i = \Lambda_{ii}\). Moreover, \(\det(\lambda I - A) = \det\big(P(\lambda I - \Lambda)P^T\big) = \prod_i(\lambda - \lambda_i)\), so these are all of the eigenvalues of \(A\).

    6. For any \(x\), let \(y = P^Tx\). Then \(|y|^2 = x^T P P^T x = |x|^2\) and \(|Ax| = |P\Lambda y| = |\Lambda y|\). As \(x\) runs over all nonzero vectors, so does \(y\), and therefore \(\|A\| = \|\Lambda\| = \max_i|\lambda_i|\) by 4 and 5.

    (The same proof works for normal matrices (\(A^*A = AA^*\)) over \(\mathbb C\), which are exactly the matrices \(A = U\Lambda U^*\) with \(U\) unitary.)

    Exercise 14.6 Compute (by hand) the PLU decomposition of \[ \begin{align}\nonumber C = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 6 & -10 \\ 15 & 0 & -6 \\ 5 & 1 & -4 \end{pmatrix} \end{align} \] using the largest pivot at each step, and check your answer with lu(C). Use your answer to compute \(\det C\).

    Step 1. The largest entry in column 1 is \(15\), so \(p(1) = 2\), \(u_1 = (15, 0, -6)\) and \(\ell_1 = (0, 1, \frac13)^\intercal\). Then \(A^{(2)} = C - \ell_1 u_1\) has rows \((0,6,-10)\), \((0,0,0)\), \((0,1,-2)\).

    Step 2. The largest entry in column 2 is \(6\), so \(p(2) = 1\), \(u_2 = (0,6,-10)\) and \(\ell_2 = (1, 0, \frac16)^\intercal\). Then \(A^{(3)} = A^{(2)} - \ell_2 u_2\) has only one nonzero entry: \(A^{(3)}_{33} = -2 + \frac{10}{6} = -\frac13\).

    Step 3. \(p(3) = 3\), \(u_3 = (0, 0, -\frac13)\) and \(\ell_3 = (0,0,1)^\intercal\). Therefore, \(p = (2,1,3)\) and \[ PL = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ \frac13 & \frac16 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{pmatrix} 15 & 0 & -6 \\ 0 & 6 & -10 \\ 0 & 0 & -\frac13 \end{pmatrix}, \] which agrees with lu(C) (F.L is \(PL\), F.U is \(U\) and F.p is \(p\)). Since \(PC = (PL)U\) and \(\det P = -1\) (one swap), \(\det C = -15 \cdot 6 \cdot (-\frac13) = 30\).

    Exercise 14.7 Let \(A\) be invertible.

    1. Show that \(\kappa(A) \geq 1\) and that \(\kappa(\alpha A) = \kappa(A)\) for all \(\alpha \not= 0\),
    2. Show that, if \(A\) is symmetric with eigenvalues \((\lambda_i)\), then \(\kappa(A) = \max_i |\lambda_i| / \min_i |\lambda_i|\),
    3. Compute \(\kappa(A)\) for \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) and check your answer with cond(A).

    1. By Exercise 14.5, \(1 = \|I\| = \|A A^{-1}\| \leq \|A\| \|A^{-1}\| = \kappa(A)\). Also \(\|\alpha A\| = |\alpha| \|A\|\) and \((\alpha A)^{-1} = \alpha^{-1} A^{-1}\), so \(\kappa(\alpha A) = |\alpha| |\alpha|^{-1} \|A\|\|A^{-1}\| = \kappa(A)\).

    2. If \(A = P\Lambda P^T\), then \(A^{-1} = P\Lambda^{-1}P^T\) is symmetric with eigenvalues \((\lambda_i^{-1})\). By Exercise 14.5, \(\|A\| = \max_i |\lambda_i|\) and \(\|A^{-1}\| = \max_i |\lambda_i|^{-1} = 1/\min_i |\lambda_i|\).

    3. The eigenvalues are \(3\) (eigenvector \((1,1)^\intercal\)) and \(1\) (eigenvector \((1,-1)^\intercal\)), so \(\kappa(A) = 3\).
